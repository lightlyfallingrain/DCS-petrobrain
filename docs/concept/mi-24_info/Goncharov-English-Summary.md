# Valentin Goncharov: Mi-24 service in Afghanistan

## English summary of the supplied Russian transcript

Source: *Армейская авиация в Афганистане. Вспоминает Валентин Гончаров* — [original interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XXeI-8LZa0A), approximately 2 hours 37 minutes. This summary is based on the transcript supplied by the user, **Mi-24 pilot interview RU transcript.txt**. It replaces the earlier, limited overview as a substantive account of the interview.

**Reading note:** The transcript appears to be automatic speech recognition. It frequently mangles aircraft designations, names, technical terms and sentence boundaries. The summary paraphrases passages whose meaning is reasonably clear, avoids uncertain numerical specifications, and flags important ambiguities. Timestamps indicate where to check the original audio. The stories are Goncharov's retrospective testimony; they have not been independently corroborated here. They describe his units and assignments, rather than universal Mi-24 practice.

## Main impression

Goncharov presents combat helicopter flying as a combination of demanding aircraft handling, close cooperation with ground troops, practical improvisation and trust between crew members. His emphasis is often less on spectacular weapon effects than on getting people inserted, supported, recovered and home safely. Heat, dust, fatigue, poor communications, maintenance errors and limited power margins repeatedly complicate missions.

For understanding the operator, the most revealing passage is around **1:24:42–1:27:05**. The commander mainly flies, while both crew members search for targets. The operator helps maintain navigation and orientation, watches for friendly special forces, searches for activity such as dust trails, and selects possible emergency landing sites. Guided-missile employment requires his participation and the commander's authorization. Near the end, Goncharov describes a good crew as one mechanism whose members understand each other without needing everything stated explicitly.

## 1. Training and entry into combat — 2:02–25:04

Goncharov grew up in a military aviation family and graduated from the Syzran helicopter school in 1981. He describes its theoretical foundation positively: navigation, tactics and aerodynamics were taught by experienced instructors. Having trained on the Mi-8, he received an additional period of theoretical instruction for the Mi-24 before joining an operational regiment at Berdychiv.

Conversion was not, in his account, an entirely new intellectual world. Many instruments and basic helicopter principles were familiar; the differences lay in systems, equipment and especially weapons. Unit-level examinations and progressively more demanding flights followed. Operators and aircraft commanders gained clearances appropriate to their respective positions. Ability, technical competence, reliability and initiative influenced progression.

His first Afghan deployment arose when a flight was required to replace combat losses. The account of his selection is conversational and partly garbled, but the personal circumstance is clear: he was about to marry, and his wedding arrangements were expedited before departure. Arrival at Bagram brought an immediate encounter with heat, altitude and unfamiliar surroundings.

Before the later deployment, mountain training at Chirchiq introduced crews to conditions closer to Afghanistan: reduced available power, different aircraft behaviour and demanding approaches and departures. He distinguishes this preparation from the experience acquired through repeated operational flying.

## 2. Working with special forces — 25:04–39:15

His later assignment centred on the 205th helicopter squadron supporting special forces across widely separated bases in southern Afghanistan. Crews had to insert or extract groups by day or night, including from hot, dusty desert sites. The combination of reduced power and dust obscuring the surface made these tasks particularly demanding.

The division of labour between aircraft was clear in the operations he describes: **Mi-8s transported the troops; Mi-24s protected them.** He treats safeguarding the transport helicopters as a matter of professional honour. This is more specific than the Mi-24's theoretical ability to carry troops in its cabin.

Ground groups were often supplied for several days. Intelligence about a caravan could delay their planned extraction, leaving them short of water and with exhausted radio batteries. When fighting began, helicopters might arrive at night with only tracer fire and agreed visual signals to interpret. He recalls troops firing signal rockets toward the enemy, allowing crews to understand the engagement when radio communication had failed. He acknowledges the obvious danger of hitting friendly troops, and attributes the system's usability to prior coordination and practice.

Helicopter pairs could rotate through refuelling and rearming while others maintained support. Extraction followed when conditions permitted. His account therefore describes an operation sustained by several crews, ground groups and logistical cycles, rather than an isolated attack run.

He also discusses captured weapons and goods. In his telling, trophies were documented and unauthorized appropriation was punishable. Organized opportunities to fire captured weapons and occasional awards of captured watches formed part of unit life. These claims remain his description of local practice.

## 3. Low-level and night flying — 39:15–50:20

Night flying altered perception: landing lights could appear closer than they were, prompting premature deceleration. He describes special-forces missions flown at extremely low altitude, sometimes only several metres above the ground, to limit observation. During insertions, Mi-8s could make several apparent landings, with dust concealing which one actually delivered the troops. The Mi-24s covered these movements.

Weapon use added its own hazards. Rocket flashes disturbed night vision, and rocket exhaust could affect engine operation. One story concerns a modification intended to permit separate firing of large S-24 rockets from the two sides. A wiring error caused both to launch together. One engine subsequently behaved abnormally and eventually stopped; he returned and landed on the remaining engine. The story links weapons integration and maintenance quality directly to flight safety.

He would have welcomed modern night-vision equipment. Instead, he recalls borrowing a handheld night-vision device from special forces and using it intermittently to inspect the terrain. This was improvised observation, not an account of routine flying with modern helmet-mounted goggles.

## 4. Ground controllers and the limits of target certainty — 50:20–59:33

Typical army-aviation tasks included convoy protection, transport support, troop insertion and evacuation, and cover for other aircraft. Convoy leaders could report attacks and mark the direction of hostile fire. Forward air controllers combined a map-based understanding of the operation with direct observation, correcting attacks as the ground situation changed.

A particularly vivid story concerns a controller using the callsign **Volna**. Enemy troops had approached very close to him, and he marked his position with orange smoke while asking for supporting fire. The helicopter crews faced the problem of placing fire near a man they were trying to save. After an alarming silence, he answered and thanked them; evacuation was then arranged. The episode is told through remembered radio exchanges, not a contemporaneous recording.

Goncharov is frank about friendly-fire risk. Ground troops might demand that fire come closer, but unguided rockets affected an area, and crews did not necessarily know how the friendly soldiers were distributed within it. He describes switching to cannon fire for more localized engagement. The central uncertainty was knowing where everyone actually was, not merely knowing where the target marker had been placed.

## 5. Experience, fatigue and command responsibility — 59:33–1:20:52

Combat service included protection and escort flights even when no weapon was fired. Goncharov resists reducing operational contribution to the simple act of shooting.

He distinguishes newly arrived pilots from those who had already served a tour. Some newcomers initially showed off; damage, forced landings and losses changed their attitude. Experienced pilots shared practical knowledge both formally and informally—for example, how to approach a dusty site without losing sight of it before touchdown. Repeated flying developed judgement and responses that theoretical preparation alone could not provide.

His special-forces unit did not have a stable peacetime working day. Missions dictated activity around the clock. Medical checks were still conducted, and he says a pilot could report being unwell and be replaced. Accommodation was basic, air conditioning unreliable and leisure intermittent.

His political and educational role also exposed him to institutional friction. He objects to awards being obstructed by Party-membership criteria and to inspections insisting on formal political instruction while exhausted crews needed sleep. His position is practical: rested crews could perform better than crews subjected to additional ceremonial obligations.

He criticizes disobedience and reckless initiative, but his treatment of fear is more nuanced. A pilot admitting to the commander that he was not confident about flying a mission might be viewed negatively by peers; nevertheless, honesty allowed the commander to make an informed decision. Concealed incapacity could endanger others.

## 6. What the Mi-24 crew actually did — 1:21:24–1:30:27

This section contains an important qualification to the general three-person crew description. **Goncharov says that in his unit the Mi-24 flight engineer was removed from the airborne crew under a directive intended to reduce casualties.** The engineer remained responsible on the ground for preparation, reporting fuel and weapons state, dispatching the aircraft, receiving it and recording faults. The Mi-8 retained its airborne flight engineer.

The resulting Mi-24 crew consisted of commander and pilot-operator. Their work was divided approximately as follows in his account:

| Commander | Pilot-operator |
|---|---|
| Mainly pilots the helicopter | Helps with navigation and orientation |
| Searches for targets alongside the operator | Searches for targets and signs of activity |
| Authorizes guided-missile firing | Participates in guided-missile aiming and launch |
| Manages demanding aircraft handling | Watches friendly troops and possible emergency landing sites |

His explanation of guided-missile sight alignment is colloquial, and the transcript uses wording that could misleadingly suggest laser illumination. It should not be treated as a precise technical explanation of the guidance system. The reliable takeaway is that employment required coordination between both seats.

Heat, fuel and weapons weight left limited takeoff margins. He describes careful rolling takeoffs and attention to rotor speed and engine indications. Returning was easier after fuel and ammunition had been expended.

Cockpit comfort was subordinate to being able to move and operate equipment. Unlike a Mi-8 crew sharing a spacious cockpit, the tandem-seated Mi-24 crew had little direct visual contact. He humorously recalls the limited view through the pedal area and the possibility of passing a sandwich that way. Cooperation was largely mediated by communication and familiarity rather than face-to-face interaction.

## 7. His attachment to the Mi-24P — 1:36:34–1:39:44; 2:21:38–2:28:23

The Mi-24P is his favourite: robust, dependable and forgiving of mistakes. He praises its fixed cannon's accuracy, recounting an occasion when the operator initially thought shots had missed a metal post before they inspected the visible damage.

Later stories reinforce his confidence in the aircraft: collecting a wounded man when transport helicopters were unavailable, and making a night landing to close an unsecured compartment door rather than abandon a support mission. These are individual recollections, not evidence that every such manoeuvre was routine or advisable.

He favours modernizing the Mi-24P with improved equipment. His judgements about newer helicopter configurations are personal preferences and should be read as such.

## 8. The grounded helicopter fight — 1:39:44–1:58:03

The most dramatic account begins with another crew's Mi-24 being forced down after a bullet damaged a hydraulic filter housing. Its original crew was evacuated, and special forces guarded the aircraft. Goncharov arrived with a technical party to assess repair and recovery.

The party had fluid and some replacement components, but lacked the damaged housing. A Mi-8 returned to base to obtain one. While waiting, Goncharov noticed approaching armed men; further attackers subsequently appeared from another direction.

He occupied the commander's cockpit, with an operator in the front seat. Unable to establish the desired military radio contact, he recalls asking an Indian civilian airliner to relay a request for help. With the engines stopped, he used the aircraft's battery-powered weapon systems to fire the cannon. Special-forces soldiers physically turned the helicopter by its tail to point the fixed gun toward the attackers; manipulating the tail also changed its elevation.

Repeated movement caused a landing-gear wheel to sink into the ground. When supporting Mi-24s arrived, he talked them onto hostile groups. After repairs, dust and the aircraft's awkward position complicated getting it free. A nearby Mi-8 crew provided external visual guidance, helping him reposition the aircraft before taking off and returning to base.

The story's significance is the interdependence of observation, communications, ground troops, maintenance specialists and other crews. His suggestion that this use of the cannon was historically unique is not independently verified here.

## 9. Threats, survival and weapons — 1:58:03–2:21:38

Goncharov's assessment of threats depends on the flight profile. He associates higher-level flight with portable surface-to-air missiles, while heavy machine guns were a major danger during his unit's low-level operations. The hydraulic-damage incident illustrates how even a small-arms hit could disable a critical system.

He discusses the Lipa infrared countermeasure unit and flares, emphasizing maintenance and cleanliness. His explanation of countermeasure physics is simplified; it should be treated as testimony about operational understanding rather than authoritative engineering documentation.

Parachutes were meaningful escape equipment when altitude and aircraft condition permitted their use. He recalls mandatory parachute training, his initial fear of jumping, and the relief after the canopy opened. Earlier he notes that extremely low flying could make wearing a parachute seem of little practical value. Rescue also depended on other aircraft locating, protecting and collecting the crew.

The weapons discussion includes cannon ammunition, unguided rockets, bombs and cluster munitions, with loads selected for the situation. He distinguishes weapons he used in training from those appropriate to the Afghan fighting he experienced. He also recalls severe machine-gun overheating during protection of extraction sites. Garbled designations and rates of fire in the transcript are deliberately not reproduced here.

He reports a frightening encounter near Pakistan with an aircraft he identifies as an F-4 Phantom. The identification and his interpretation of the aircraft's intent are not corroborated. Bird strikes were another recurring hazard, including one that damaged a recently replaced engine and led to a single-engine landing.

## 10. Crew cohesion and the human aftermath — 2:28:23–2:35:00

Asked who had the hardest time, he first names parents, spouses and children waiting at home. He also acknowledges the burdens carried by cooks, technicians and others whose work supported flying.

His closing description of the crew is especially relevant: mutual resentment or distrust makes an ineffective crew; commander and operator should function as a single coordinated mechanism and understand one another with few words.

He associates returning veterans with a sharpened sense of justice and resentment at unfairness or neglect. His later comments move into criticism of contemporary inequality and treatment of veterans. On the Soviet war itself, he stresses the experience gained in equipment, tactics and personnel. That is his retrospective framing; it does not by itself establish that the intervention was necessary or justified, nor does it represent an Afghan civilian perspective.

## Implications for Petrobrain — interpretation, not interview testimony

1. **The operator contributes an independent picture of the situation.** Navigation, friendly positions, observable activity and potential landing sites are directly supported by his account.
2. **Communication should reflect the pilot's workload.** The account supports a useful collaborator who contributes what the commander needs, while much of the commander's attention remains on flying. It does not supply a complete set of standard cockpit phrases.
3. **Uncertainty matters.** The ground-controller stories distinguish a marker, an enemy direction and a known friendly position. These are not interchangeable facts.
4. **Crew familiarity is central.** His strongest statement concerns mutual understanding. Plausible dialogue should emerge from a shared mission picture, rather than repeatedly restating information both crew members know.
5. **Model the specific unit and mission.** His two-person Mi-24 practice and special-forces support role should not be generalized to every Soviet or Ukrainian crew.

The transcript does contain remembered radio dialogue and a brief pilot–operator exchange, but it is not an extensive corpus of recorded intercom communication. Most dialogue is narrated retrospectively. Any expanded Petrovich dialogue derived from it should be labelled as adaptation rather than quotation.
